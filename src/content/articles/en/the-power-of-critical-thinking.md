Critical thinking is usually sold as a temperament: some people have it, the rest do not, and the rest should learn to defer. That framing makes it useless, because a temperament cannot be exercised on demand.

It is better understood as a procedure. A procedure can be learned, run on a schedule, and applied to arguments you find entirely persuasive. That is a lower bar and a much more useful one.

## Take the argument apart

Most claims arrive as a conclusion wearing a coat. The first move is to take the coat off and find the structure underneath.

A defensible argument has four parts:

| Part | The question |
|------|-------------|
| **Claim** | What exactly is being asserted? |
| **Evidence** | What is offered in support? |
| **Warrant** | Why does this evidence support this claim, and not something else? |
| **Qualifier** | How much of the time, and under what conditions? |

Most people check the evidence and stop. The warrant is where the argument actually lives, and it is almost never stated.

A number is evidence. "Sixty percent of our users did X" is evidence. "Therefore the product should be rebuilt around X" is a claim. The gap between them is a warrant — and it is where the disagreement is, whether or not anyone says so.

The qualifier is where bad arguments hide. "Students perform better with morning classes" might mean students who chose morning classes, or students who already sleep well, or a two-percent difference that vanished in a second semester. The qualifier is what makes a claim honest.

> Ask "compared to what?" more often than you ask "is this true?".

## The biases that actually do damage

Biases are usually listed as a dozen named creatures. Here are four that account for most of the damage, with the sentence each one produces.

| Bias | What it sounds like |
|------|---------------------|
| **Confirmation** | "You can see right here in the comments that people agree with me." |
| **Anchoring** | "It was already a hundred milliseconds, so making it a hundred and fifty is fine." |
| **Availability** | "My cousin went through exactly that, so it must be common." |
| **Dunning–Kruger** | "I have watched four talks on this, so I basically know the field." |

Look at the anchoring example, because it is the most revealing. The first number encountered becomes the reference point, and every later number is judged against it — not against what the thing is actually worth. This is why "we already have a hundred milliseconds" feels like a reason.

And notice the availability example. It is the one that explains most of what circulates on social platforms, and it is the reason a vivid story beats a careful study in every feed ever built.

## A procedure

This is the framework. It runs in about ten minutes and it applies to any claim you are about to accept, repeat, or act on.

1. **State the claim in your own words.** If you cannot restate it, you do not understand it yet, and you are not in a position to evaluate it.
2. **Identify what would count as evidence.** Before looking. This step is what keeps step 3 honest.
3. **Find the actual source.** Not the summary of the summary. How many people, over how long, measured how?
4. **Look for the warrant.** Why does this evidence support this particular claim?
5. **Name the alternative.** What else could produce this same observation? At least one, ideally the one your own side would be embarrassed by.
6. **Find the qualifier.** How much, how often, for whom, and since when?

If you cannot complete step 2, stop. A claim with no imaginable evidence is not a claim about the world; it is a statement of preference wearing a factual costume.

## Where it fails

Two failure modes, both common, both worth naming.

**The cost of always questioning.** A person who treats every claim as suspect buys skepticism at the price of action. Some things are simply fine — the dentist is the dentist, the seatbelt works, the bus arrives at the time it says. Skepticism that cannot be switched off is not rigor, it is an inability to commit.

**Being right is not the same as being useful.** The strongest form of this is the person who wins every argument and changes nothing. A critique that leaves you with no better option than the one you rejected has not done any work. A good argument should leave you with something to do differently.

There is also a social version of this, and it is the harder one. Critical thinking is easy alone and expensive in a group, because in a group the cost of disagreeing is not the same as the cost of being wrong. Most bad arguments are not won by people who were more convinced; they are won by people who could afford to hold the position.

> The goal is not to be right all the time. It is to be less wrong over time, and to know which one you are currently doing.

## What it looks like in practice

Three habits, all cheap:

- **Restate before you react.** Say the claim back. Most bad arguments collapse at this step, because nobody actually held the version you are arguing with.
- **Ask for the base rate.** "Out of how many?" turns a startling number into an ordinary one, or reveals that it is not.
- **Write down what would change your mind.** Not "I am open-minded" — a specific result. If you cannot name one, you are not evaluating, you are defending.

## The takeaway

Critical thinking is not a talent and not a mood. It is a fixed sequence: restate the claim, decide what evidence would count, find the source, examine the warrant, name an alternative, and pin down the qualifier.

Run it on the arguments you agree with first. Those are the ones it will feel strange to apply to, and they are where you will learn whether you have a procedure or just a preference.
