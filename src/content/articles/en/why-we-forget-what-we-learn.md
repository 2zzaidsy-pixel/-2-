Eight books, and three points survived out of ten. That is not a memory problem. It is a normal consequence of how the brain works: it deletes everything it does not need.

## The brain does not store, it rebuilds

Memory is not an archive you pull from as it was. Every time you retrieve an experience, the brain reconstructs it from small pieces. This is why details shift from one attempt to the next: the story you tell today differs slightly from the one you told last year, and both feel correct to you.

Memory is a rebuild, not a pull from a shelf. And every time you retell it, you write a new version of what you assume was stored.

> What you have kept is the last version you wrote, not every version that passed through you.

## Why it deletes

Keeping everything that passes through you is expensive. The brain chooses what serves it, and the criterion it uses is not topical importance but **the probability that you will need it in the future**.

This is the part that stings in practice: what you learn and never use is deleted faster than you expect, even when it matters in other circles of your life.

## Three conditions that keep information alive

### 1. Active retrieval

Reading is not learning. Learning starts when you try to retrieve without looking at the source:

- Close the source and restate the idea in your own words.
- Explain it to someone, or write it down.
- Apply it to a case you did not read about.

Every successful retrieval strengthens the path back, and makes the next one faster.

### 2. Encoding it in a different context

What you learn in one context is easy to lose at the first change of setting. The place you learned it is easy to recall only there.

The fix: attach every new idea to something you already know well. A new idea is stored through a network, not in isolation. This is why a single example is worth ten definitions.

### 3. Spacing

Back-to-back revision produces a false feeling of understanding. Distributing it over separated intervals is stronger:

| Day | Task |
|-----|------|
| 1 | Learn |
| 3 | Quick retrieval |
| 7 | Practical application |
| 30 | Restate it in a new context |

The condition is not the number of reviews, but the distance between them.

## Why we sometimes keep what we do not want

Memory is not a neutral archive. What carries strong emotion is stored more easily: surprise, frustration, fear. This is why the details of a bad day stay sharper than the details of an ordinary one, even though the ordinary day mattered more.

Behavior itself is subject to a negative pull stronger than the positive one: **loss cuts deeper than gain, roughly speaking**. Breaking a bad habit in six months is about as hard as building a good one in six months, and starting is the stronger part.

## What to do about it

1. **Shorten what you keep** — five points, not fifty.
2. **Write it** — a written idea becomes retrievable; a heard one does not.
3. **Connect it** — never leave an idea isolated from a living context.
4. **Revisit it** — at intervals, not in one sitting.
5. **Retrieve it** — cover the source and try to remember before you look.

## The takeaway

You do not forget what you failed to memorize; you fail to use it, and the brain deletes what goes unused. The answer is not to try harder at memorization, but to build a path that gets the knowledge called back: retrieval, context, and spacing.

Start with one thing: pick a single idea from whatever you read last, close everything, and rewrite it in three sentences. If you cannot, you do not own it yet.
