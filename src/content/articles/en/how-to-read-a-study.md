A headline is not evidence. Thousands of studies are published every week, and only a few survive inspection. The distance between being told something and understanding it starts with the first question: where did this number come from?

## Start with the sample

Before the results, ask about the people the study included:

- How many, and from which country?
- How were they chosen: at random, or through volunteers and followers?
- Did the study cover the whole group, or only a part of it?

A study of two hundred followers on one particular platform says nothing about people in general, even when its conclusion claims the results may apply to other communities.

> The sample limits what can be concluded, however careful the statistics.

## One effect is not a discovery

A single study that found a large difference is not a new discovery. In research, one effect in one paper stays a candidate result until it replicates.

Watch for these phrases:

- "New research shows that..."
- "Science proves that..."
- "A small laboratory study found that..."

**Science proves**, then **one study suggests**, then **one lab study hints**. Three different degrees of certainty, sometimes written in the same tone of voice.

## Effect size, not sample size

A large sample with a tiny effect means a change nobody notices in their own life. A moderate sample with a clear effect may matter more. When you read the results, look for:

- The size of the effect, not just the number of participants.
- Whether the result was still there a year or two later.
- Whether it replicated in different settings.

Results that evaporate after a few months are a sign of the effect's limit, not of broken research.

## Who is behind the study

Before you believe it, ask three questions about the source:

1. **Who funded the study?** Interests are not cancelled, but they have to be declared.
2. **Was it published in a peer-reviewed journal?** And more important: what did the reviews say.
3. **Has anyone published a response?** Research that provokes no discussion needs a reason.

## The takeaway

You do not need a degree in methodology to read a good paper. You need eight lines at the end of any scientific article:

- Where did the sample come from?
- How big is the effect?
- Who funded it?
- Has the result replicated?

If you cannot find the answer to a question in the text, that absence is itself the answer.

The last step: if you cannot explain the result in one sentence, in your own words, do not publish it and do not believe it.
